The sky, once, fell. So the question is fair — could it happen to us?

Start with one.

That single glowing point is one asteroid — a real rock in a real orbit. Earth is the blue sphere at the center, and the faint tail is the asteroid's path relative to us.

When one glows red…

…it's flagged potentially hazardous — big enough and close enough in orbit to be worth watching. Hold that thought. It is the most misunderstood word in this whole story.

Now meet all 2,000 of them.

Every dot is one near-Earth asteroid, each on its true orbit, all swirling around the Earth at the center. This is the entire neighborhood — the population we're about to put on trial.

533 of them wear that red label.

More than a quarter of the population. It sounds like a verdict — but it's only a watch-list rule: bigger than ~140 m, and orbit passing within 0.05 AU of ours.

It means worth tracking — not predicted to hit. Watch the red trails. Every one bends past Earth.

And "close" is an astronomical word.

Across 13,691 approaches this century, the typical one misses by 79 lunar distances — nearly 79 times farther than the Moon.

The chart on the right is that whole distribution. Sweep across its bars and the matching asteroids light up in orbit. Almost everything lives far, far out.

The close ones are small.

A dinosaur-scale strike needs a rock that is both huge and on our doorstep. Plot size against closest pass, and that corner is simply empty.

The true giants — Ganymed, Eros — sit far to the right, never approaching. Click any point and the scene flies to it.

So what would it actually take?

Nearly every one of them is big enough to flatten a city. About 1,286 could threaten a civilization. Just 11 match the dinosaur-killer in size.

Size is only one bar, though. The bar that matters — an actual collision course — is one that none of the 2,000 clear.

We even host rocks bigger than the dinosaur-killer.

Ganymed is ~87 km — seven times Chicxulub — and never crosses our path. Of the ones that do come near, the largest is Florence at ~9 km, missing by 7.5 million km.

The dinosaur-killer stands alone — bigger than the close ones, closer than the big ones, and the only one that ever actually hit.

Which brings us to Apophis.

In 2004 it earned the highest impact odds ever recorded — about 2.7% for 2029. Then more data arrived, the orbit tightened, and the odds for the next century fell to zero.

On 13 April 2029 it still sweeps just 38,000 km past Earth — closer than our own satellites, bright enough to see by eye. Watch the distance fall, then climb away.

The dinosaurs never had this. We do — and so far, every rock we've measured is just passing through.

So — should we be worried?

The honest, data-backed answer.

The dinosaurs never saw it coming. We have catalogued 2,000 of our nearest neighbors, projected ~42,000 of their encounters with Earth, and found a population that misses us by a median of 79 lunar distances — whose giants stay away and whose close passers stay small. The one object that ever truly scared us, Apophis, we de-escalated from record-setting threat to naked-eye spectacle using nothing but patience and better data. The sky fell once. We are now very sure we'll see it coming if it ever tries again.

Initial Prototype Writeup

1. What have you done so far?

Our team, The Pickle Farmers, finalized our dataset (NASA's Near-Earth Asteroids set), built a data pipeline that cleans the 2,000-object catalog, derives each asteroid's time-propagation elements, and flattens ~42,000 Earth close-approach events. On top of it we built a Three.js "Orbital Theater" that propagates every asteroid through a century with Kepler's equation — and rebuilt the whole explanation as a scrollytelling experience where that 3-D scene is the persistent centerpiece: the narrative scrolls past it and drives it, flying the camera to specific asteroids, toggling the hazardous set, and surfacing scene-linked D3 charts at each beat. Selecting any asteroid grades it live against the impact and extinction thresholds.

2. What will be the most challenging part of your project to design and why?

The hardest part is the seamless coupling between the scrolling 2-D narrative and the live 3-D scene: propagating thousands of orbits every frame without dropping frames, choreographing camera transitions from a macro view down to a single close approach, and keeping the simulation clock, the chart overlays, and the live annotations all in sync as the reader scrolls. Keeping the user oriented through those dynamic, scroll-driven camera moves is the central UX challenge, and it has taken heavy iteration.
