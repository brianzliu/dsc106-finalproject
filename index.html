The Chicxulub impact, and the question it leaves behind.

Sixty-six million years ago, an asteroid between ten and fifteen kilometers across struck the shallow sea off what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula. It hit with the force of billions of nuclear weapons. The impact hurled a curtain of vaporized rock around the planet, ignited wildfires across continents, and drew a veil of soot across the sky that blotted out the Sun for years.

In the long winter that followed, photosynthesis stalled, food chains collapsed, and an estimated three-quarters of all species on Earth went extinct — among them the non-avian dinosaurs that had ruled the planet for 165 million years. It is the most consequential few seconds in the history of life on Earth, and the clearest proof we have that the sky can, on rare occasion, fall.

So the anxiety is reasonable. Every time a "potentially hazardous asteroid" makes the news, the same quiet question resurfaces: it happened to them — could it happen to us? This is an attempt to answer that question honestly, using nothing but the real orbital records of the 2,000 asteroids that share Earth's neighborhood. The conclusion, assembled live in the scene below, is reassuring — and the reasons why are worth seeing for yourself.

That's 27% of them, now glowing red. The label sounds like a verdict. It isn't.

It's a watch-list rule: any object bigger than ~140 m whose orbit passes within 0.05 AU of Earth's. It means worth tracking — not predicted to hit. Watch where the red trails go. They bend past Earth, every time.

What an asteroid must clear to matter

Nearly all of them are big enough to ruin a city. About 1,286 could threaten a civilization. Just 11 rival the dinosaur-killer in sheer size.

But size is only one bar. The other — being on a collision course — is one that zero of our 2,000 clear.

Apophis — the scare that proved the system

In 2004 it earned the highest impact odds ever recorded: ~2.7% for 2029. More data arrived, the orbit tightened, and the odds for the next century fell to zero.

On 13 April 2029 it still passes just 38,000 km away — inside our own satellites, naked-eye bright. The scene is flying there now; watch the live distance fall.

So — should we be worried?

The honest, data-backed answer.

The dinosaurs never saw it coming. We have catalogued 2,000 of our nearest neighbors, projected ~42,000 of their encounters with Earth, and found a population that misses us by a median of 79 lunar distances — whose giants stay away and whose close passers stay small. The one object that ever truly scared us, Apophis, we de-escalated from record-setting threat to naked-eye spectacle using nothing but patience and better data. The sky fell once. We are now very sure we'll see it coming if it ever tries again.

Initial Prototype Writeup

1. What have you done so far?

Our team, The Pickle Farmers, finalized our dataset (NASA's Near-Earth Asteroids set), built a data pipeline that cleans the 2,000-object catalog, derives each asteroid's time-propagation elements, and flattens ~42,000 Earth close-approach events. On top of it we built a Three.js "Orbital Theater" that propagates every asteroid through a century with Kepler's equation — and rebuilt the whole explanation as a scrollytelling experience where that 3-D scene is the persistent centerpiece: the narrative scrolls past it and drives it, flying the camera to specific asteroids, toggling the hazardous set, and surfacing scene-linked D3 charts at each beat. Selecting any asteroid grades it live against the impact and extinction thresholds.

2. What will be the most challenging part of your project to design and why?

The hardest part is the seamless coupling between the scrolling 2-D narrative and the live 3-D scene: propagating thousands of orbits every frame without dropping frames, choreographing camera transitions from a macro view down to a single close approach, and keeping the simulation clock, the chart overlays, and the live annotations all in sync as the reader scrolls. Keeping the user oriented through those dynamic, scroll-driven camera moves is the central UX challenge, and it has taken heavy iteration.
