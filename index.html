The story begins off the coast of what is now Yucatán, where a mountain of rock arrived at roughly twenty kilometers a second.

The impact threw dust and soot into the atmosphere. Sunlight dimmed, temperatures fell, and the effects moved through ecosystems over the years that followed.

The difference now is observation. The sky is no longer unmeasured.

The question is not whether asteroids pass near Earth. They do. The question is what those passages look like when distance, size and trajectory are put in the same frame.

The threshold is practical rather than apocalyptic: roughly larger than 140 m, with an orbit that comes within 0.05 AU of Earth’s orbit.

That label is the beginning of monitoring, not the end of analysis. It says the object is worth watching; it does not say the object is going to hit us.

In astronomy, close still leaves room.

Across 13,691 projected approaches this century, the typical pass misses by 79 lunar distances. That is far outside the Moon’s orbit.

The close passers tend to be small.

A dinosaur-scale impact requires two things at once: a very large object and a trajectory that reaches Earth.

When size and closest approach are plotted together, the largest objects sit far away, while the nearest passers are much smaller.

The thresholds separate scale from trajectory.

Many objects in the sample are large enough to matter if they struck Earth, and a much smaller group is large enough to have global consequences.

But the final threshold is not size. It is an actual collision course. None of the 2,000 objects in this view crosses that line.

Some nearby asteroids are enormous.

The comparison on the right is a scale check. Some near-Earth asteroids are larger than the Chicxulub impactor, while others are closer or better known.

Those facts have to be read separately. Size, closeness and an Earth-crossing path are different ingredients of risk.

Apophis is why early numbers need context.

When Apophis was discovered in 2004, astronomers had only a short arc of observations. The first orbit estimates left enough uncertainty that a 2029 impact could not immediately be ruled out.

That uncertainty was temporary. More observations narrowed the orbit, and the risk estimates changed with the measurements.

The 2029 flyby is real. The impact is not.

With better data, the 2029 impact risk disappeared, and later measurements ruled out an impact for at least the next century.

On 13 April 2029, Apophis will still pass about 38,000 km from Earth. The scene follows that close flyby as the distance falls and then grows again.

So, should we be worried?

What the measurements show.

The catalog is not a promise that impacts can never happen. It is a measurement system, and that distinction matters. In this sample of 2,000 near-Earth asteroids, the projected approaches miss by a median of 79 lunar distances. The largest objects stay distant, the closest passers are small, and Apophis shows why repeated observations matter: better measurements can turn an early concern into a well-understood flyby.

Initial Prototype Writeup

1. What have you done so far?

Our team, The Pickle Farmers, finalized our dataset (NASA's Near-Earth Asteroids set), built a data pipeline that cleans the 2,000-object catalog, derives each asteroid's time-propagation elements, and flattens ~42,000 Earth close-approach events. On top of it we built a Three.js "Orbital Theater" that propagates every asteroid through a century with Kepler's equation — and rebuilt the whole explanation as a scrollytelling experience where that 3-D scene is the persistent centerpiece: the narrative scrolls past it and drives it, flying the camera to specific asteroids, toggling the hazardous set, and surfacing scene-linked D3 charts at each beat. Selecting any asteroid grades it live against the impact and extinction thresholds.

2. What will be the most challenging part of your project to design and why?

The hardest part is the seamless coupling between the scrolling 2-D narrative and the live 3-D scene: propagating thousands of orbits every frame without dropping frames, choreographing camera transitions from a macro view down to a single close approach, and keeping the simulation clock, the chart overlays, and the live annotations all in sync as the reader scrolls. Keeping the user oriented through those dynamic, scroll-driven camera moves is the central UX challenge, and it has taken heavy iteration.
