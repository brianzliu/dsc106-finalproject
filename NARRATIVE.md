### A data narrative on the near-Earth asteroids — and why none of them are coming for us

## The Hook — 66 million years of silence, broken once

Sixty-six million years ago, a rock between **10 and 15 kilometers** across slammed into the shallow sea off what is now the Yucatán Peninsula. The Chicxulub impactor hit with the energy of billions of nuclear weapons, threw a curtain of vaporized rock into the sky, and plunged the planet into a years-long impact winter. Roughly **three-quarters of all species on Earth died** — and with them, the non-avian dinosaurs that had ruled the planet for 165 million years.

It is the single most cinematic fact in Earth's history: the sky, once, fell. And every time a "potentially hazardous asteroid" makes the news, the same question surfaces — quietly at first, then louder:

> **It happened to them. Should we be worried that it happens to us?**

This narrative is an honest answer to that question, built entirely from the orbital records of 2,000 real near-Earth asteroids. The short version: **the fear is reasonable, but the data is reassuring.** Let us show you why.

---

## Thesis — The big ones stay away, and the close ones are small

Two facts do almost all the work in calming the dinosaur-extinction fear, and both are visible directly in our dataset.

**1. The dinosaur-killer was in a different size class entirely.**
Chicxulub was 10–15 km across. In our catalog of 2,000 near-Earth objects, the largest bodies are genuine giants — **1036 Ganymed at ~87 km** and **433 Eros at ~50 km** — but here is the crucial detail: *they are not classified as hazardous.* They belong to the **Amor** orbit class, asteroids that approach Earth's orbit from outside but never cross it. The biggest rocks in the neighborhood keep a respectful distance. The objects that *do* swing close to us are, almost without exception, hundreds of meters wide — city-threatening at worst, never civilization-ending.

**2. "Close" in astronomy is still astronomically far.**
Of the **13,691 Earth close approaches** our dataset projects for **2026–2125**, the *median* miss distance is **78.8 lunar distances** — that is, the typical "close" approach still passes nearly 79 times farther away than the Moon. The distribution has a long, comfortable tail:

| Miss distance | Share of 2026–2125 approaches |
|---|---|
| Inside the Moon's orbit (< 1 LD) | **3 events (0.02%)** |
| Within 5 LD | 49 events (0.4%) |
| Within 10 LD | 180 events (1.3%) |
| Within 20 LD | 656 events (4.8%) |
| **Median** | **78.8 LD** |

Read that top row again: across an entire century, only **three** of nearly fourteen thousand projected passes dip inside the Moon's own orbit — and not one of them is on a collision course.

**Potentially hazardous ≠ on a collision course.** Of our 2,000 asteroids, **533 (27%)** carry NASA's "potentially hazardous" label. That label is a *watch-list* definition — it flags any object larger than ~140 m whose orbit passes within 0.05 AU (~19.5 lunar distances) of Earth's. It means "worth tracking carefully," not "predicted to hit." Every single one of the 533 is, on the orbits we have, a near miss.

This is the scatter that kills the dinosaur fear directly. If a Chicxulub-scale impact were a live threat, we would expect to find large objects crowding the left edge of the plot (close approaches). Instead the largest bodies sit far to the right — Ganymed and Eros never come near us — while the only objects that hug the left edge are small. **There is no asteroid in our data that is both large enough to threaten civilization *and* on a trajectory that brings it dangerously close.** The dangerous quadrant — big *and* close — is empty.

## Case Study — 99942 Apophis, the asteroid that proves the rule

If any object in our catalog deserves a dramatic close-up, it is **99942 Apophis (2004 MN4)** — named, fittingly, for the Egyptian god of chaos.

When Apophis was discovered in 2004, early observations gave it the highest impact-probability rating any asteroid had ever received: for a brief, alarming stretch, astronomers calculated a **~2.7% chance** of an Earth impact in 2029. It climbed to Level 4 on the Torino impact-hazard scale — unprecedented. For a few days, it looked like the question this narrative opens with might have a frightening answer.

Then more observations arrived, the orbit tightened, and the impact probability for 2029 — and, after a 2021 radar campaign, for the next **century** — fell to **zero**. Apophis is now one of the most precisely tracked objects in the solar system.

Here is what makes it the perfect case study: **Apophis is still going to be spectacularly close.**

- **Date of closest approach:** 13 April 2029
- **Miss distance:** **0.10 lunar distances — about 38,000 km**
- **For scale:** that is *inside* the geostationary satellite belt (~35,800 km altitude). Apophis will pass *beneath* some of our own communications satellites.
- **Size:** ~340–370 m across (our dataset's max-diameter estimate is ~900 m; refined radar puts it near 340 m) — large enough to flatten a region, nowhere near Chicxulub's 10–15 km.
- **Visibility:** it will be visible to the naked eye from Europe, Africa, and western Asia — a slow-moving star, briefly.

Apophis is the asteroid that *sounds* like the trailer for a disaster movie — record-setting initial odds, a god-of-chaos name, a pass closer than our own satellites — and it is, on the numbers we now have, a **guaranteed miss**. It is the single best demonstration of the system working: a real scare, detected years in advance, refined by observation, and resolved to certainty. After 2029, our records carry Apophis through dozens more passes across the next century, the next closest being a comfortable **6.8 LD in 2116**.

The lesson of Apophis is not that we got lucky. It is that we *looked*, and looking is enough.

## Conclusion

Should we be worried that what happened to the dinosaurs happens to us?

The dinosaurs never saw it coming — they had no telescopes, no orbit solvers, no watch-list. We have catalogued 2,000 of our nearest neighbors, projected ~42,000 of their encounters with Earth, and found a population that misses us by a median of 79 lunar distances, whose giants stay away and whose close passers stay small. The one object that ever truly scared us, Apophis, we de-escalated from "record-setting threat" to "naked-eye spectacle" using nothing but patience and better data.

The sky fell once. We have spent the last few decades making very sure we will see it coming if it ever tries again — and so far, every rock we have measured is, on the orbits we have, just passing through.
